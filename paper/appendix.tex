\textbf{Decade.} Six indicators (1960s \dots\ 2010s), exactly one equal to 1. On ISMIR, the release year as
a number; on SpotGenTrack, the release decade as a number (1990 for 1990--1999).

\textbf{Artist history.} Defined in Sec.~\ref{sec:features} and Fig.~\ref{fig:history}. On the Hit Predictor
and the chart, artist names are processed in this order: accents removed (Unicode NFKD decomposition, then
non-ASCII characters dropped) and lower-cased; cut at the first
separator, which must be written with a space on each side, i.e.\ a whole word (so even the comma counts only
as `` , ''; ``Earth, Wind \& Fire'' is cut at `` \& '' into ``earth wind''), among (``featuring'', ``feat.'', ``ft.'', ``with'', ``\&'',
``x'', ``+'', ``/'', ``vs.'', ``and'' or a comma; so ``Brandy'' is not cut); every character other than a--z, 0--9
and space removed, and spaces collapsed. So ``Madonna feat.\ Justin Timberlake'' counts for Madonna.
On the Hit Predictor the reference set is the weekly Hot~100 since August 1958 (so the 1958--59 charts form a
``1950s'' decade that counts as earlier for 1960s songs): for every artist and every
decade (the decade of the chart week), we collect the set of distinct lower-cased titles, and $h(s)$ sums the
sizes of these sets over the decades strictly before the song's decade. On ISMIR and SpotGenTrack it is the set
of positive songs of the dataset, counted over strictly earlier release years. $\ln$ is the natural logarithm.

\textbf{Rejected candidates.} \emph{Song title}: TF-IDF of word uni- and bigrams, logistic regression,
out-of-fold probability as one feature. \emph{Title length}: number of words. \emph{chorus\_hit,
sections}: two fields of the Hit Predictor computed from Spotify's audio-analysis endpoint (inherited from the
Echo Nest, the music-analysis company Spotify bought), the estimated
start time of the chorus (the author's estimate: the start of the third section) and the number of sections (verse, chorus, \dots) of the track.

\emph{Audio from the 30-second previews.} Every preview is decoded to mono at 22{,}050\,Hz (first 30\,s). All
frame-based descriptors are computed with librosa~0.11~\cite{mcfee2015librosa}, an open-source Python library for
audio analysis, with its defaults: a short-time Fourier transform with a 2{,}048-sample window
(93\,ms) and a hop of 512 samples (23\,ms). \emph{FFT} (31; the name only says that every descriptor is derived from
the fast Fourier transform of the clip, i.e.\ its spectrogram): mean and standard deviation over frames of the
spectral centroid, bandwidth, roll-off (85\,\%), flatness and contrast (contrast averaged over its 7 frequency
bands, so one value per frame); the mean of each of 13 MFCCs; the mean and standard deviation of the 12-bin
STFT chroma, taken over all bins and frames together (two numbers); and six rhythm descriptors: tempo estimated from the onset envelope, mean and
standard deviation of the onset envelope, \emph{pulse clarity} (the maximum divided by the mean of the
time-averaged tempogram, i.e.\ how strongly one periodicity dominates), and the mean and coefficient of variation
(s.d./mean) of inter-onset intervals. \emph{Rich librosa} (185): the mean and standard deviation over frames
of each row of 20 MFCCs and of their first and second derivatives (120), 12 constant-Q chroma bins (24), 6 tonnetz
dimensions of the harmonic part (12), 7 spectral-contrast bands (14), spectral centroid, bandwidth and roll-off
(6), flatness (2), zero-crossing rate (2) and RMS energy (2), plus the onset-envelope mean and standard deviation
and the pulse clarity (3); reduced to 40 principal components inside each training fold.

\emph{Genre} (16 families, control only). Spotify artist tags (from the SpotGenTrack artist file, matched by
artist name normalised as on the Hit Predictor; when a name occurs twice, the artist with more followers is kept) are mapped by
substring to families: hip-hop (hip hop, rap, trap, drill, grime, crunk, bounce), R\&B/soul (r\&b, soul, funk,
motown, disco, new jack, quiet storm, doo-wop), country (country, bluegrass, americana, honky, western swing), rock
(rock, punk, grunge, emo, britpop, new wave, shoegaze, post-), metal (metal, hardcore, deathcore, grindcore, thrash,
doom), electronic (edm, house, techno, trance, electro, dubstep, drum and bass, dance, idm, ambient, downtempo,
breakbeat, glitch, synth), pop (pop, boy band, girl group, teen, adult standards, easy listening), jazz (jazz,
bebop, swing, big band, bop, exotica, lounge), classical (classical, orchestra, baroque, opera, romantic era, early
music, choral, compositional, minimalism, soundtrack, score), folk (folk, singer-songwriter, acoustic, cantautor),
blues, Latin (latin, reggaeton, salsa, bachata, cumbia, mexican, tango, bossa, samba, mpb, sertanejo, flamenco,
ranchera, banda, tropical), reggae (reggae, dancehall, ska, dub, rocksteady), gospel (gospel, christian, worship,
ccm, spiritual), world (afro, k-pop, j-pop, indian, bollywood, arab, turkish, greek, celtic, world, chanson,
schlager, french, german, italian, brazil, polka, nordic, russian, african, hawaiian), and other (no match). A tag is assigned to every family one of whose keywords it contains (so ``rocksteady'' counts for rock and
reggae, ``k-pop'' for pop and world). An artist gets one yes/no input per family; the \emph{dominant} family is the
one matched by the most tags (ties go to the family listed first above), and it is used for the
genre-matched sample, in which, for every dominant family (``other'' included), we draw at random (seed 42) as
many hits as flops (the smaller of the two counts).

\emph{Market trend.} Descriptors are standardised over the whole dataset. For each decade we compute the
centroid (mean vector) of its hits. A song's \emph{distance} is the Euclidean distance between its descriptors and
the centroid of the previous decade's hits; its \emph{direction} is the projection of that difference on the
unit vector pointing from the centroid two decades back to the one of the previous decade (dot product divided by
the vector's length); the 13 \emph{deviations} are the
coordinates of the difference. The 1960s have no previous decade (distance and deviations set to the median and
to 0), and the direction exists from the 1980s on (median before). These features are computed once on the whole
dataset, from earlier decades' hits only, not inside the folds; since they add nothing, this does not affect any
conclusion.

\textbf{Lyrics} (ISMIR). From musiXmatch word counts, which cover only the 5{,}000 most frequent stemmed words
(so all counts below are over that vocabulary, not the full text): $\ln(1+{}$number of words$)$, $\ln(1+{}$number of
distinct words$)$,
richness (distinct/total), repetition (count of the most frequent word/total), shares of \emph{I, me,
my}, of \emph{you, your}, of \emph{love}, and of 29 common English words, matched as written against the stemmed vocabulary (28 of them occur in it;
\emph{don't} does not and is ignored) (\emph{the, i, you, and, to, a, me,
my, it, in, of, is, that, your, on, be, we, all, love, do, so, no, for, what, with, can, this, but, don't}); plus
the out-of-fold TF-IDF logistic-regression probability. A song counts as English if at least 25\,\% of its words
are in that list.

\textbf{Early trajectory.} A ``song'' is one title--performer pair of the weekly chart file. Week $w$ is the
chart published $w-1$ weeks after the debut chart; $p_w$ is the song's position in it (101 = not on the chart).
For $k$ weeks: the $k$ positions, the best of these $k$ positions, the number of these $k$ weeks in which the
song is on the chart (nothing after week $k$ is used), the slope
$(p_k-p_1)/(k-1)$ (negative = climbing; only for $k\geq2$) and the acceleration $(p_k-p_{k-1})-(p_{k-1}-p_{k-2})$
(only for $k\geq3$); context: debut year and month, $\ln(1+{}$earlier songs of the artist, i.e.\ songs that debuted before this one$)$ and
$\ln(1+{}$earlier songs of the artist that had already reached the top~10 before the debut$)$, the artist being
the performer field normalised as on the Hit Predictor. The ``not yet top~10'' column of Table~\ref{tab:traj} comes
from a model retrained, with the same settings, on those songs only. Permutation importance
(Fig.~\ref{fig:traj}c) uses one stratified 75/25 split (seed 42). The target is
whether the song's peak over its whole chart run is in the top~10; songs are kept only if they debuted after the
first chart (August 1958) and at least 52 weeks before the last one, so that their run is almost always complete
(a song that peaks for the first time more than a year after its debut is rare).

\section{Time-series details}\label{app:ts}

The chart analysis of Sec.~\ref{sec:timeseries} is in a notebook (\texttt{timeseries/time\_series\_hot100.ipynb})
that follows the standard sequence of a time-series study~\cite{hyndman2021forecasting}: plot the series,
decompose it into trend, seasonality and residual, look for cycles with the periodogram, model the cycles
with Fourier terms, test stationarity (ADF, KPSS) and autocorrelation (ACF, PACF, Ljung--Box), and compare
forecasting methods by rolling origin. Test settings (statsmodels defaults otherwise; computed on the whole
1959--2025 series): ADF with the lag order chosen
by AIC; KPSS around a constant with automatic lag; Ljung--Box at lags 12 and 24. On the monthly debut series, ADF
does not reject a unit root ($p=0.51$) and KPSS rejects stationarity ($p=0.01$); after one difference ADF rejects
($p<0.001$) and KPSS does not ($p\geq0.10$), hence $d=1$ (fixed from the whole series, forecast windows
included; the orders $p,q$ are chosen on pre-origin data only). The STL residual is close to white noise (Ljung--Box does
not reject at lags 12 and 24).

\textbf{ARIMA in one paragraph.} ARIMA$(p,d,q)$ models a series by differencing it $d$ times until it has
no trend, then explaining each value from its own $p$ previous values (autoregression) and from the $q$
previous forecast errors (moving average). We chose $d=1$ from the stationarity tests and $(p,q)$ by AIC
over $p,q\leq2$, on the data before the first origin only. Adding Fourier terms as regressors captures yearly
seasonality with few parameters.

\textbf{Forecasting settings.} Series: monthly mean number of debuts per chart week. Rolling origin: 11 origins,
every January from 2015 to 2025; at each origin the models are fitted on all earlier months and forecast the next
12. STL: period 12, robust fitting. ARIMA$(1,1,2)$; ARIMA + Fourier: the same with $K=2$ yearly harmonics as
regressors; seasonal ARIMA: $(1,1,2)(P,0,Q)_{12}$ with $P,Q\leq1$ chosen by AIC; ETS: additive damped trend and
additive seasonality of period 12. Gradient boosting (300 trees, depth 3, learning rate 0.03, subsample 0.8) and
random forest (300 trees, at least 5 samples per leaf) predict the monthly change $y_t-y_{t-1}$ from its 12 lags,
the last level, the 12-month rolling mean and $K=2$ Fourier terms of the month, and forecast 12 months recursively
(each prediction is fed back as a lag); seed 42. MASE scales the mean absolute error by that of the seasonal naive
forecast on the training months.

\section{Glossary}\label{app:glossary}
{\footnotesize\setlength{\parskip}{2pt}
\noindent\textbf{Bag of words / TF-IDF.} A text is represented by how often each word occurs, ignoring order. TF-IDF weights a
word by its (sublinear) frequency in the text, $1+\ln\mathrm{tf}$, times $\ln\frac{1+N}{1+\mathrm{df}}+1$, where $N$ is
the number of texts and df the number containing the word (the scikit-learn default), so that words found
everywhere count little; each text vector is then scaled to length 1. A logistic regression on these weights gives a hit probability.\par
\noindent\textbf{Bootstrap sample.} A sample of $n$ songs drawn \emph{with replacement} from $n$ songs; about 63\,\% of them
appear at least once. Each tree of a Random Forest is grown on its own bootstrap sample.\par
\noindent\textbf{ACF, PACF.} Autocorrelation function: correlation of a series with itself $\ell$ steps earlier, for each lag
$\ell$; the partial version removes the effect of the shorter lags.\par
\noindent\textbf{ADF, KPSS.} Two stationarity tests (a series is \emph{stationary} if its mean and variance do not drift). ADF
assumes a unit root (the series behaves like a random walk, each value being the previous one plus a shock, so it
is non-stationary) and tries to reject it; KPSS assumes stationarity and tries to reject it.\par
\noindent\textbf{AIC.} Akaike information criterion: goodness of fit penalised by the number of parameters; lower is better.\par
\noindent\textbf{Anti-conservative.} A test is anti-conservative when it gives $p$-values that are too small, i.e.\ it declares
``significant'' more often than it should.\par
\noindent\textbf{Chroma, tonnetz.} Energy of each of the 12 pitch classes (C, C\#, \dots, B) in a frame, computed from a
short-time Fourier transform (STFT chroma) or from a constant-Q transform, whose frequency bins are spaced like
musical notes (CQT chroma); tonnetz projects chroma on harmonic relations (fifths, thirds) and is computed on the
\emph{harmonic part} of the signal, obtained by separating sustained (harmonic) from percussive sounds.\par
\noindent\textbf{ISMIR.} International Society for Music Information Retrieval; ``ISMIR~2019'' is the dataset released with
Zangerle et~al.'s paper at its 2019 conference.\par
\noindent\textbf{Essentia.} An open-source library for audio analysis; the ISMIR release provides its low-level (spectral,
rhythmic, tonal) and high-level (mood, genre classifier outputs) descriptors.\par
\noindent\textbf{ETS / damped Holt--Winters.} Exponential smoothing: the forecast is a weighted average of past values with
weights that decrease geometrically, with a level, a trend that flattens out (damped) and a seasonal part.\par
\noindent\textbf{Cross-validation, fold, stratified.} See Sec.~\ref{sec:protocol}.\par
\noindent\textbf{Fourier terms.} $\sin(2\pi jt/m)$ and $\cos(2\pi jt/m)$ for $j=1,\dots,K$, where $m$ is the season length (12
months, or 52.18 weeks) and $K$ the number of harmonics; a regression on them draws any smooth yearly pattern.\par
\noindent\textbf{Gini impurity.} $1-q^2-(1-q)^2$ for a group with hit share $q$; a split is chosen to lower it the most.\par
\noindent\textbf{Ljung--Box test.} Tests whether the first $L$ autocorrelations of a series are all zero (white noise).\par
\noindent\textbf{Logistic regression.} A linear model whose weighted sum of the inputs is turned into a probability by
$1/(1+e^{-z})$; $C$ is the inverse strength of the L2 penalty on the weights.\par
\noindent\textbf{$k$-NN.} Predicts the majority answer of the $k$ most similar training songs.\par
\noindent\textbf{MFCC.} Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients: a compact description (13 or 20 numbers per frame) of the shape of
the sound spectrum on a perceptual frequency scale; standard for timbre.\par
\noindent\textbf{Million Song Dataset (MSD).} A public collection of metadata and audio features for one million songs.\par
\noindent\textbf{Onset envelope, tempogram.} The onset envelope measures, frame by frame, how much the spectrum suddenly
increases (note attacks); the tempogram is its local autocorrelation, showing which beat periods are present.\par
\noindent\textbf{One-hot encoding.} A category with $m$ values becomes $m$ yes/no inputs, exactly one of which is 1.\par
\noindent\textbf{Out-of-fold.} A value for a training song produced by a model that did not see that song (inner split).\par
\noindent\textbf{PCA.} Principal component analysis: new axes, combinations of the inputs, ordered by how much of the variance
they explain.\par
\noindent\textbf{Periodogram.} The squared magnitude of the Fourier transform of a series: how much variance each period
(cycle length) explains.\par
\noindent\textbf{Permutation importance.} Accuracy (or AUC) lost when one input is shuffled across the test songs.\par
\noindent\textbf{RBF SVM.} A support vector machine with a radial (Gaussian) kernel.\par
\noindent\textbf{Seasonal naive.} Forecast equal to the value observed one season (12 months) earlier.\par
\noindent\textbf{Standardisation.} Replacing each input by (value $-$ mean) / standard deviation, the mean and standard
deviation being computed on the training part.\par
\noindent\textbf{Stemming.} Reducing a word to its stem with fixed suffix rules (Porter stemmer: ``causes'' $\to$ ``caus'',
``future'' $\to$ ``futur'', while ``love'' stays ``love''); the musiXmatch release is stemmed this way.\par
\noindent\textbf{STL; trend and seasonal strength.} Seasonal--trend decomposition by local regression (Loess): series = trend
+ seasonal + residual. Trend strength is $\max(0,1-\mathrm{Var}(R)/\mathrm{Var}(T+R))$ and seasonal strength the same
with $S$ in place of $T$ (0 = none, 1 = everything).\par
\noindent\textbf{Spectral centroid, bandwidth, roll-off, flatness, contrast.} Centre of mass of the spectrum; its spread; the
frequency below which 85\,\% of the energy lies; how noise-like it is; the difference between peaks and valleys
in each frequency band.\par
\noindent\textbf{Track URI.} Spotify's unique identifier of a track (\texttt{spotify:track:\dots}).\par
\noindent\textbf{TreeSHAP, $|\phi|$.} For a tree model, $\phi$ is how much an input moves one prediction away from the average
prediction (SHAP value); the mean of $|\phi|$ over songs ranks the inputs.\par
\noindent\textbf{Wide-and-deep.} A neural network that combines a linear part on the raw inputs with a deep part (used by
Zangerle et~al.).\par
\noindent\textbf{XGBoost.} Gradient-boosted trees: trees are added one by one, each correcting the errors of the previous ones.\par
}

\section{Questions a reader may ask}\label{app:faq}

\textbf{Is artist history not cheating?} No information from the song's own period or later is used
(Fig.~\ref{fig:history}), so it is available on release day. What inflates its gain on the Hit Predictor
is the construction of the negatives (artists of hits are excluded), which is why we report the
year-matched ISMIR gain ($+2.6$\pp) as the transferable number.

\textbf{Why does the paper say the gain holds for unseen artists but not for new artists?} The two words
mean different things (end of Sec.~\ref{sec:stats}): an unseen artist is absent from the training folds but may
have earlier hits; a new artist has none, so $h(s)=0$.

\textbf{Why Random Forest and not XGBoost?} XGBoost is 1.3 points better under cross-validation but ties
on the canonical split ($p=0.87$); the frozen Random Forest keeps our numbers comparable with the published
configuration, and the gain of our features is the same for all five classifiers (Table~\ref{tab:models}).

\textbf{Why no deep learning?} The question is which inputs carry signal, at equal model. A learned audio
representation could capture what the descriptors miss, and is future work.

\textbf{Why reject the song title when it is the strongest feature?} A title does not make a song a hit.
Its gain comes from formatting (``- Remastered'', ``feat.'') that only non-hit titles carry, and from genre and
language words; once both are controlled, what remains ($+1.9$\pp, $p=0.002$) is only indicative, below the Bonferroni threshold,
and is itself mostly genre and language vocabulary (Appendix~\ref{app:audit}).

\textbf{Why does release year alone score below chance (0.448) in ISMIR protocol~B?} Every hit has a
non-hit from the same year, so the year carries no information, and a Random Forest on a single input with
many distinct values fits noise in the training folds; with exactly balanced years, what it learns for a
year is then, on average, the opposite of the test fold's labels for that year, which pushes it below 0.5.

\textbf{Why is FFT rejected although its gain is significant on the whole benchmark?} Because it vanishes
when hits and non-hits come from the same genres (Table~\ref{tab:genre}): spectral descriptors recognise the
niche genres from which the benchmark draws its non-hits.

\textbf{Why are the canonical numbers lower than the cross-validated ones?} The canonical split trains on
20{,}000 songs, cross-validation on 32{,}448; with more training data every configuration is about one point
better, and the differences between configurations are the same.

\textbf{Why are lyrics kept but not genre, if lyrics also partly reflect genre?} Spotify genre tags
describe the artist as known today, so they are not known on release day.
The words of a song are known on release day. On ISMIR the negatives are random catalogue songs, so the
difference in themes between chart songs and the rest is real rather than built into the dataset; we
nevertheless state that lyrics measure mainstream-ness more than the appeal of one song
(Sec.~\ref{sec:lyrics}).
