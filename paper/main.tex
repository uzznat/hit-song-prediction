\title{What Really Predicts a Hit?\\ Artist History, Era Context and Shortcut Features\\ in Hit Song Prediction}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Lior Shalon, Nathanael Uzzan}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Technion -- Israel Institute of Technology, Haifa, Israel\\
Project in Artificial Intelligence}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Can a computer tell, on the day a song is released, whether it will enter the Billboard Hot~100, the weekly
ranking of the 100 most popular songs in the United States? Published
studies report 82--89\,\% accuracy from Spotify audio descriptors, but on different datasets, usually on a single
train/test split, and without checking whether the datasets themselves give the answer away. We build a simple,
fully described pipeline: 13 audio descriptors, the song's decade, and the artist's \emph{history} (how many Hot~100
songs the artist placed in earlier decades), fed to a Random Forest, and we test every gain with
cross-validation (repeated wherever affordable), a corrected $t$-test and a Bonferroni correction (a few
robustness checks use single splits and McNemar's test). On the 40{,}560-song Spotify Hit Predictor
dataset, accuracy rises from 0.782 (audio only) to 0.832; the only earlier model on that dataset improves on 50 of 50
random splits when the feature is added. Because that dataset, by its construction rule, never uses the artists of
a decade's hits as that decade's negatives, we
validate on ISMIR~2019, where a year-matched test gives the transferable estimate: $+2.6$ points. An audit of every
other input we built shows which gains are not real: song titles, spectral descriptors and release year score
well only because of how the datasets were assembled. Lyrics are the one content input whose gain survives
year matching, an English-only test and unseen artists ($+4.4$ points), and once a song is out, its first three weeks on the chart predict a top-10 peak with an
AUC of 0.89. All details are in the appendix; all code and data are public.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Whether a song will become a hit is a question with obvious commercial value and a long,
contested research history. Early work concluded that ``hit song science is not yet a
science''~\cite{pachet2008hit}, later work argued the opposite on other
data~\cite{ni2011hit}, and the field has since moved from hand-crafted audio descriptors
to convolutional networks on spectrograms~\cite{yang2017revisiting} and multimodal models
combining audio, lyrics and metadata~\cite{zangerle2019hit,martingutierrez2020multimodal,nofer2026deephits}.

A large share of recent studies relies on the audio descriptors exposed by the Spotify
Web API and on labels derived from the Billboard Hot~100
chart~\cite{middlebrook2019song,dimolitsas2023spothitpy,zhao2023analysis,du2022spotify}.
Three weaknesses make these results hard to interpret. First, papers report accuracies on
\emph{different} datasets, so numbers such as 86\,\%, 88\,\% or 89\,\% are not
comparable. Second, gains are usually measured on a single train/test split without a
significance test. Third, the datasets are assembled with sampling rules that a model can
exploit: a feature may separate hits from non-hits because of how the negative class was
chosen rather than because of anything that makes a song successful, an instance of data
leakage~\cite{kaufman2012leakage} and shortcut learning~\cite{geirhos2020shortcut}.

This paper studies two cheap, non-audio signals---the artist's prior chart history and
the song's era---under an evaluation protocol designed to address these three weaknesses.
Artist history is not a new idea. Interiano et~al.~\cite{interiano2018musical} add a
``superstar'' indicator (whether the artist charted in the recent past) to acoustic
features of 500{,}000 UK releases and find that it raises Random-Forest accuracy, and
Middlebrook and Sheik~\cite{middlebrook2019song} list an artist past-performance field
among chart-derived fields, although the inputs of their final model are not stated.
Neither study tests the gain for significance, compares it with prior models on the same
data, or asks whether it reflects the way the negative class was sampled. What we add is a version restricted to strictly earlier periods, a
significance-tested measure of its gain on the same data as prior work, and a check on an
independent dataset of whether that gain is a genuine signal or a sampling artefact.
Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A leakage-free formulation of \emph{artist history} which, together with era, improves every
classifier we tested on the Spotify Hit Predictor benchmark (Sec.~\ref{sec:main}).
\item A \emph{head-to-head} comparison with the only academic work on that benchmark, using
its exact protocol, in which our feature improves its models on 50 out of 50 random splits
(Sec.~\ref{sec:tilburg}).
\item An \emph{external validation} on the peer-reviewed ISMIR~2019 dataset, including a
year-matched control that separates a genuine signal from a sampling artefact, and in which
our feature exceeds the best published accuracy on the original protocol (protocol A, with a classifier
instead of their regression and our own draw of negatives; Sec.~\ref{sec:external}).
\item A \emph{robustness} analysis showing that the gain is not the model recognising artists it was
trained on (artist-grouped cross-validation), together with a cold-start breakdown showing that, on the
Hit Predictor, artist history does not help artists with no earlier hit and only era does (on ISMIR with
year-matched negatives there is a hint that an empty history is informative, not significant after Bonferroni;
Sec.~\ref{sec:robust}).
\item An \emph{ablation} of the final model and a model trained on every input at once, showing where
the accuracy comes from (Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation}).
\item An \emph{audit} showing which apparent gains do not survive: title-based features that
encode dataset construction, and a rich audio representation whose single-split gain
disappears under repeated cross-validation (Sec.~\ref{sec:audit}), and a genre control
showing that a significant spectral-feature gain on the whole benchmark is a genre
shortcut (Sec.~\ref{sec:further}).
\item \emph{Lyrics}: on ISMIR~2019 with year-matched negatives, the bag of words of a song's lyrics adds $+4.4$ to
$+5.1$ points ($+4.4$ in the strictest test; with random negatives $+2.7$ to $+4.7$, indicative only) and survives year matching, an English-only control and unseen artists, the only content input
to do so (Sec.~\ref{sec:lyrics}).
\item A \emph{time-series} analysis of the Hot~100: components and forecasting of chart
dynamics, market-trend features before release, and early chart trajectories after release
(Sec.~\ref{sec:timeseries}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Hit prediction.} Pachet and Roy~\cite{pachet2008hit} found that audio could not predict popularity, while
later studies reported above-chance results on chart data~\cite{ni2011hit,herremans2014dance}, with convolutional
networks~\cite{yang2017revisiting} or multimodal models of audio, lyrics and metadata~\cite{zangerle2019hit,
martingutierrez2020multimodal,nofer2026deephits}. Studies built on Spotify descriptors and Billboard labels report
86--89\,\%~\cite{middlebrook2019song,dimolitsas2023spothitpy,zhao2023analysis}, each on its own dataset; the only
academic study on the public Spotify Hit Predictor dataset~\cite{ansari2020dataset} is Du's thesis~\cite{du2022spotify}
(84\,\% and 82\,\%), and the best result on the peer-reviewed ISMIR~2019 data (released at the 2019 conference of the International
Society for Music Information Retrieval) is 75.04\,\%~\cite{zangerle2019hit}.
\textbf{Artist reputation.} Interiano et~al.~\cite{interiano2018musical} showed that a ``superstar'' indicator helps
predict UK chart success, and Salganik et~al.~\cite{salganik2006experimental} that success feeds on itself.
\textbf{Evaluation.} Single-split comparisons need McNemar's test~\cite{dietterich1998approximate}; repeated
cross-validation needs a corrected $t$-test~\cite{nadeau2003inference}. Models can exploit how a dataset was built
rather than the task (leakage~\cite{kaufman2012leakage}, shortcut learning~\cite{geirhos2020shortcut}); in music,
Sturm~\cite{sturm2014simple} and Flexer~\cite{flexer2007closer} showed this for genre recognition. A longer review is
in Appendix~\ref{app:related}.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Overview: what goes in, what comes out}
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarises the whole method. The task is binary classification: \emph{input}, one
song described by 20 numbers that are all known on the day it is released (13 audio descriptors, six decade
indicators and the artist's history); \emph{output}, the probability that the song enters the Billboard
Hot~100, turned into a yes/no answer at 0.5. The model is a Random Forest, an ensemble of decision trees that
each ask a sequence of threshold questions about the inputs (``is energy above 0.7?'', ``did the artist
have an earlier hit?'') and then vote. The same pipeline is run on three datasets, each with its own audio
descriptors. Every claimed gain is measured as the difference in held-out accuracy between the pipeline with
and without an input, under the protocol in the middle of the figure. A second, separate pipeline (bottom of the
figure) answers a different question that can only be asked after release.

Fig.~\ref{fig:algorithm} opens the box: how the forest is trained, and one real song followed from its raw data to
the final answer.

\subsection{Features}\label{sec:features}
\textbf{Audio.} The 13 Spotify descriptors (Hit Predictor), the 592 Essentia descriptors
(ISMIR) or the same 13 Spotify descriptors (SpotGenTrack; App.~\ref{app:inputs}) form the baseline of each dataset.

\textbf{Decade (era).} The Hit Predictor comes as six files, \texttt{dataset-of-60s.csv} to
\texttt{dataset-of-10s.csv}; a song's decade is simply the file it comes from: for a hit, the decade in which it
was on the Hot~100; for a non-hit, the decade the dataset's author assigned it to (the documentation does not
say more; App.~\ref{app:data}). A decade is a category, not a quantity, so we turn it into six yes/no inputs (one-hot encoding:
\texttt{is\_60s}, \dots, \texttt{is\_10s}), exactly one of which equals~1 (Fig.~\ref{fig:algorithm}, step~2b).
Because every file is balanced, the decade alone says nothing about whether a song is a hit (0.503 accuracy, at
chance level: always answering ``non-hit'' gives 0.506). It helps because \emph{what a hit sounds like changes with the era} (Fig.~\ref{fig:decade}): a 1990s hit is
louder than a 1960s hit, and the median 2000s non-hit is louder than the median hit of any decade from the 1960s
to the 1980s; in the 1960s--90s hits are
less acoustic than non-hits, in the 2000s slightly more; in the 1970s--90s hits are longer than non-hits, in the
2000s--10s shorter. With the decade as input, the trees can learn a different rule for each era. ISMIR and
SpotGenTrack have no decade files; there the era is one number, the release year on ISMIR (e.g.\ 1987) and the
release decade on SpotGenTrack (e.g.\ 1990, from the album release date). On ISMIR we also test the decade,
the year rounded down to ten.

\textbf{Artist history.} Let $a(s)$ be the normalised primary artist of song $s$ (on the Hit Predictor and for
the chart history: lower-cased, accents removed, cut at the first collaborator separator such as ``feat.'',
``\&'', ``with'' or a comma, each written with a space on both sides (full list in App.~\ref{app:inputs}), then punctuation removed; on ISMIR and
SpotGenTrack, whose artist fields are cleaner: only lower-cased and trimmed, on ISMIR the whole artist field, on
SpotGenTrack the first artist of its artist list) and $t(s)$ its period (decade on
the Hit Predictor, release year elsewhere). With $\mathcal{H}$ the set of reference hits, each element $s'$ being
one (artist, title, period) entry, so that a title charting in two decades gives two entries,
\begin{equation}
h(s) = \ln\bigl(1 + \bigl|\{\,s' \in \mathcal{H} : a(s') = a(s),\; t(s') < t(s)\,\}\bigr|\bigr).
\end{equation}
The strict inequality guarantees that no information from the song's own period or from
the future is used. One subtlety remains: a hit whose chart run crosses a decade boundary, or that re-entered the
chart, also appears among the artist's titles of the previous decade, and so counts itself. This concerns 219
songs (211 hits, 0.5\,\%); excluding the song's own title from the count leaves the result essentially unchanged (0.831
instead of 0.832; still $+5.0$\pp{} over audio alone, 25/25 folds, $p=4\times10^{-18}$; \texttt{r8\_future\_work.py selfcount}; this check
was run on the Hit Predictor only), so we keep the simpler definition. In words: for every song, we look up its artist in the full Hot~100
history and, for each decade before the song's own decade, count how many different songs that artist had on
the chart in that decade, then add these counts (a song that charted in two decades counts once in each); the natural logarithm $\ln$ compresses large counts (an artist with 40 earlier hits is
not twice as likely to chart again as one with 20). Fig.~\ref{fig:history} gives a worked
example. The value is computed separately for every artist and every period, so the same
artist gets a larger value in each later decade, and an artist with no earlier hit always gets
$h(s)=0$.

\input{fig_history} On the Hit Predictor, $\mathcal{H}$ is the full weekly Hot~100 history
(distinct titles per artist and decade, the decade being that of the chart week); on ISMIR and
SpotGenTrack it is the set of positive songs of the dataset itself, counted by earlier release year. On the Hit
Predictor, 52.1\,\% of songs have an artist that appears somewhere in the Hot~100 history: 99.99\,\% of the hits
and 5.4\,\% of the flops. The unmatched songs are therefore flops by artists who never charted, not matching
failures (Sec.~\ref{sec:further}).


\subsection{Models}
The main model is the Random Forest~\cite{breiman2001random} configuration of Middlebrook
and Sheik~\cite{middlebrook2019song} (80 trees, 8 candidate features per split, or all of them for a block with
fewer than 8 inputs; splits chosen by Gini impurity, Fig.~\ref{fig:algorithm}),
kept frozen throughout: we do not tune hyperparameters, so that gains come from the
features and remain comparable with the published configuration. Robustness checks use XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost}, logistic
regression, $k$-NN and an RBF support vector machine (each defined in App.~\ref{app:glossary}). External datasets use a Random Forest
with 200 trees and $\sqrt{d}$ candidate features per split ($d$ = number of inputs), the usual default when $d$
is large (592 descriptors on ISMIR). All models are the scikit-learn~1.8 and XGBoost~3.2 implementations
(Python~3.14); audio features use librosa~0.11.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
Every model is always tested on songs it did not see during training (\emph{held-out} songs). The main
comparisons on the Hit Predictor are reported with two estimates; most other experiments use cross-validation
only, and a few robustness checks use single splits (stated where they appear).

\emph{Cross-validation (the estimate our conclusions rest on).} The songs are shuffled and cut into five equal
parts (\emph{folds}), each with the same share of hits (\emph{stratified}). The model is trained on four parts
(32{,}448 songs on the Hit Predictor) and tested on the fifth (8{,}112 songs); this is repeated so that every part
is the test part once, giving five accuracies. The whole procedure is then repeated five times with a
different shuffle, giving $5\times5=25$ test accuracies (``$5{\times}5$ CV''; scikit-learn
\texttt{RepeatedStratifiedKFold}, seed 42). Two configurations, e.g.\ with and without artist history, always use
the \emph{same} 25 splits, so they can be compared fold by fold. Anything learned from data (standardisation, PCA,
the text models) is fitted on the training part only. Two exceptions: the rejected market-trend features
(App.~\ref{app:inputs}), and artist history on ISMIR and SpotGenTrack, which counts the dataset's own earlier hits,
including songs that fall in the test folds; this uses only information from strictly earlier years, so it is
legitimate in time. Where one run is expensive we use fewer repeats:
$5{\times}2$ on ISMIR (592 descriptors per song) and $5{\times}1$ on SpotGenTrack (101{,}909 songs). Here
``$5{\times}2$'' always means 5 folds repeated twice, not the 2-fold ``$5{\times}2$cv'' design of
Dietterich~\cite{dietterich1998approximate}.

\emph{Canonical split (a second, independent check).} One fixed split, seed 42: from the 40{,}560 songs, a
sample of 24{,}000 is drawn and divided into 20{,}000 training, 2{,}000 validation (not used) and
2{,}000 test songs by three successive scikit-learn \texttt{train\_test\_split} calls, each stratified by label with
seed 42: 24{,}000 songs kept out of 40{,}560, then 20{,}000 of them for training, then the other 4{,}000 cut into
2{,}000 validation and 2{,}000 test songs. This was the split of the first version of the study and we keep it unchanged so that its
numbers can be compared over time; it uses fewer training songs, which is why its accuracies are about one point
lower. On ISMIR and SpotGenTrack the canonical split is a stratified 75/25 split.

\emph{Out-of-fold text features.} For the song title (and later the lyrics), a TF-IDF + logistic-regression model
(App.~\ref{app:glossary}) is trained on the training part only, with an inner stratified, shuffled 5-fold split (seed 42) so that every training song
gets a probability from a model that did not see it; the test songs get the probability of the model trained on the
whole training part. That probability is then used as one input.

\subsection{Metrics and statistics in plain words}\label{sec:stats}
\textbf{Metrics.} \emph{Accuracy} is the share of test songs whose answer (hit or non-hit) is right. A gain in
\emph{points} (pts) is a difference in accuracy times 100: going from 0.782 to 0.832 is $+5.0$\pp. On the hit
class, \emph{precision} is the share of songs predicted ``hit'' that are hits, \emph{recall} the share of hits that
are predicted ``hit'', and \emph{F1} their harmonic mean $2PR/(P+R)$. \emph{ROC-AUC} (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve) uses the predicted probability
instead of the yes/no answer: it is the probability that a randomly chosen hit gets a higher score than a randomly
chosen non-hit (0.5 = chance, 1 = perfect); it is useful when classes are imbalanced (SpotGenTrack), where always
answering ``non-hit'' already gives high accuracy. \emph{Wins} (e.g.\ 25/25) is the number of the 25 folds in which
the configuration with the new input is more accurate than without it (a tie does not count as a win).

\textbf{What a $p$-value says.} Suppose the new input were in fact useless. Its measured gain would then be
pure noise, sometimes positive, sometimes negative. The $p$-value is the probability of seeing a gain at least as
large in absolute value as the one we measured if the input were useless. A small $p$ (e.g.\ $10^{-10}$) means
that such a gain would almost never appear by chance; ``n.s.'' (not significant) means $p\geq0.05$.

\textbf{The test we use (corrected resampled $t$-test~\cite{nadeau2003inference}).} For each of the $J$ folds we
compute the paired difference $d_j$ = accuracy with the input $-$ accuracy without it (or the same difference of
AUC or F1 when those are tested), then its mean $\bar d$ and
its sample variance $s^2$ (here $s$ is a standard deviation, not a song). A naive paired $t$-test would use $t=\bar d/\sqrt{s^2/J}$. But the 25 folds are not
independent: any two training sets share most of their songs, so the 25 differences vary less than truly
independent estimates would, and the naive test finds ``significant'' gains far too easily. Nadeau and Bengio
correct this by inflating the variance:
\begin{equation}
t=\frac{\bar d}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{J}+\frac{n_{\text{test}}}{n_{\text{train}}}\right)s^2}},
\end{equation}
where $n_{\text{test}}/n_{\text{train}}$ is the ratio of test to training songs in one fold ($8{,}112/32{,}448 = 1/4$
in 5-fold CV, $3/7$ for 70/30 splits). The $p$-value is two-sided, from a Student $t$ distribution with $J-1$
degrees of freedom. \emph{Worked example} (Table~\ref{tab:main}, audio vs.\ audio + decade + artist): over the 25
folds the gain has mean $\bar d=0.0504$ and standard deviation $s=0.0045$ (smallest 0.041, largest 0.058). The
naive test would give $t=56$; the corrected one gives $t=0.0504/\sqrt{(1/25+1/4)\times0.0045^2}=20.8$, and with 24
degrees of freedom $p=7\times10^{-17}$. The per-fold accuracies of every experiment are in the repository
(\texttt{paper\_results/*\_folds.csv}), so every $p$-value can be recomputed.

\textbf{Confidence intervals.} The 95\,\% interval printed next to an accuracy is the mean of the $J$ fold
accuracies $\pm\,t_{0.975,J-1}\,s_{\text{acc}}/\sqrt{J}$, where $s_{\text{acc}}$ is the standard deviation of the $J$
fold accuracies and $t_{0.975,J-1}$ the 97.5\,\% quantile of a Student $t$ distribution with $J-1$ degrees of
freedom (2.06 for $J=25$). It is not corrected for the overlap of folds, so it
describes how stable the 25 estimates are and is narrower than the true uncertainty; significance is always
judged with the corrected test above, never from the intervals.

\textbf{Single split: McNemar's test~\cite{dietterich1998approximate}.} On one test set, we count $b$, the songs only
the old model gets right, and $c$, the songs only the new model gets right. If the two models were equally good, $b$
and $c$ would be about equal; the test statistic is $(|b-c|-1)^2/(b+c)$, compared with a $\chi^2$ distribution
with one degree of freedom (an exact binomial test when $b+c<25$).

\textbf{Many tests at once (Bonferroni).} With many tests, some small $p$-values appear by chance alone: among 100
useless inputs, about five would reach $p<0.05$. Bonferroni divides 0.05 by the number of tests. We do not rely on
an exact count: every $p$-value printed in the paper that compares two configurations, counting a test again each time it is printed, plus the
exploratory tests we ran but do not print (App.~\ref{app:stats}), stays well below 400, and we
divide by 400, a deliberately generous family size. A result is therefore called significant after Bonferroni only if $p<0.05/400=1.25\times10^{-4}$;
results with $1.25\times10^{-4}\leq p<0.05$ are called \emph{indicative}. Inputs are judged on the accuracy
gain unless a row says otherwise (so the SpotGenTrack lyrics stay indicative on accuracy, $p=0.002$, although their
AUC gain, $p=2\times10^{-5}$, would pass).

\textbf{Rounding.} Gains $\Delta$ are computed from unrounded accuracies, so a gain can differ by 0.1\pp{} from the
difference of the rounded values printed in a table (e.g.\ $0.8103-0.7817=0.0286$, printed $+2.9$, although $.810-.782=.028$).

\textbf{Two kinds of ``new'' artist.} Two different situations are easy to confuse, and we keep
them apart throughout. An \emph{unseen} artist is one whose songs are absent from the training
folds; its chart history before the song is still known, so $h(s)$ can be positive. We test unseen
artists with artist-grouped cross-validation (Sec.~\ref{sec:robust}), to check that the model does
not simply recognise artists it was trained on. A \emph{new} (cold-start) artist is one with no
earlier hit in the reference set $\mathcal{H}$ (the full Hot~100 on the Hit Predictor; on ISMIR and SpotGenTrack only
the dataset's own hits, so an ISMIR ``new'' artist may have Hot~100 songs that are not in the dataset), so $h(s)=0$
by definition. Our claims that the gain ``holds for unseen artists'' never mean that it helps new artists. A value
of 0 could still carry information, since the model learns from the known artists what an empty history means. On
the Hit Predictor it does not ($+0.4$\pp, n.s.; only era helps new artists); on ISMIR with year-matched negatives
there is a hint that it does ($+1.4$\pp, $p=0.002$, above the Bonferroni threshold, so indicative only;
Table~\ref{tab:robust}).

\subsection{How inputs were selected}\label{sec:selection}
\input{fig_selection}
We did not choose the inputs in advance. Every candidate we built---decade, artist history, song title,
title length, \texttt{chorus\_hit}/\texttt{sections} (two analysis fields of the dataset; App.~\ref{app:inputs}), a
185-dimensional representation computed with the audio library librosa, 31 ``FFT'' descriptors (spectral and
rhythm statistics of the spectrogram of a 30-second preview), Spotify artist genres, market-trend features and lyrics---went through the same four
questions (Fig.~\ref{fig:selection}). We first ask whether the input is known on release day. We then
measure its gain under cross-validation ($5{\times}5$; $5{\times}2$ on ISMIR; a single $5$-fold run on
SpotGenTrack) with the corrected $t$-test, not on one split. We
then re-test it under the controls that remove the known construction shortcuts of each dataset: negatives
matched by year (ISMIR), negatives matched by genre (Hit Predictor), and no artist shared between training
and test folds. Each control is applied separately (the title also gets a combined genre + formatting control,
Sec.~\ref{sec:auditmain}); an input \emph{fails} when, under at least one of the controls we ran on it, its gain is
no longer significant after Bonferroni. A genre-matched \emph{and} artist-grouped run at once was not done. On ISMIR, protocol A
(random negatives) contains the release-year shortcut, so ISMIR inputs are judged on the year-matched protocol B,
with the other controls (English only, unseen artists) applied on top of it; protocol-A numbers are reported for
comparison with prior work. Finally we require significance after a
Bonferroni correction over all the tests in the paper. We call an input that passes all four questions a \emph{surviving} input, and we rank all tested gains
by this criterion in Table~\ref{tab:survival} (the \emph{survival ranking}). Artist history survives on the Hit
Predictor and on ISMIR; on SpotGenTrack its gain is only an upper bound, because popularity is measured today
(Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}). The decade survives on the Hit Predictor only (where it matters through its interactions with audio);
on ISMIR, once years are matched, the era adds nothing (Sec.~\ref{sec:external}). Bag-of-words lyrics survive on the one
dataset where we could attach them to both hits and non-hits, ISMIR (Sec.~\ref{sec:lyrics}); on SpotGenTrack only
six lexical descriptors are available, and their gain is indicative. We also trained
a model on everything at once, and we removed one input at a time from both that model and the final model
(Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\subsection{Where the accuracy comes from}\label{sec:ablation}

\textbf{Which audio descriptors matter.} A principal component analysis of the 13 descriptors
(Appendix~\ref{app:ablation}, Fig.~\ref{fig:pca}) finds an \emph{intensity} axis (energy, loudness against
acousticness) and a \emph{mood} axis (danceability, valence), but the directions of largest variance are not the
ones that separate hits, and replacing the descriptors by their components only loses accuracy ($-1.7$\pp{} with all
13), so we keep the raw descriptors.


\textbf{Ablation: remove one input at a time.} To see where the power of the final model comes from, we
removed each of its inputs in turn and re-ran the $5{\times}5$ cross-validation, comparing every reduced
model with the full one fold by fold (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}a). Removing artist history costs the most
($-3.0$\pp, 0/25 folds better, $p=7\times10^{-13}$), then the decade indicators ($-2.2$\pp,
$p=5\times10^{-13}$), instrumentalness ($-1.6$\pp, $p=6\times10^{-9}$) and duration ($-1.1$\pp,
$p=2\times10^{-7}$). These four survive the Bonferroni correction. Acousticness, speechiness, energy,
danceability and valence each cost 0.3--0.6\pp{} ($0.004\leq p\leq0.04$), because the other descriptors
largely carry the same information. Removing loudness ($-0.3$\pp), key, liveness, time signature, mode or tempo changes nothing
($p\geq0.30$). Keeping one block alone shows that the blocks are complementary rather than redundant: the
13 descriptors alone reach 0.782, decade and artist history together without audio 0.670, the decade alone
0.503 (chance, since every decade is balanced), and all three together 0.832. Removing all 13 descriptors at once costs $-16.2$\pp.
So audio carries most of the accuracy, and artist history and era add to it.

\textbf{Permutation importance.} Removing an input lets the model re-learn with the others, so
correlated descriptors can stand in for one another. As a second view, we shuffle one input at a time in the
held-out folds of the trained final model (the 5 folds of the first repeat, 5 shuffles each), which measures how
much the model relies on it
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}b). The ranking is the same at the top: artist history (8.9\pp{} lost when shuffled),
then instrumentalness (6.3), acousticness (4.9), the decade block (4.3; its six indicators are shuffled together), duration (3.9) and danceability
(3.4), with key, liveness and time signature near zero. Duration is an instructive case. On its own it barely
separates hits (AUC 0.51; Appendix~\ref{app:inputs}), but the model relies on it, presumably because song lengths
changed across decades and its effect depends on the era. Its value comes from interactions, like that of the
decade.

\subsection{Unseen artists and cold start}\label{sec:robust}

\textbf{Unseen artists.} With random folds, songs by the same artist can be both in training and in
test, so a model could recognise an artist from the audio. With folds grouped by artist, so that no test artist is
ever seen in training, every gain remains: $+5.0$\pp{} on the Hit Predictor and $+2.9$\pp{} in the year-matched ISMIR
test ($p=9\times10^{-7}$). \textbf{New artists.} For songs whose artist had no earlier hit, artist history is 0 and
does not help on the Hit Predictor ($+0.4$\pp, $p=0.17$); the decade still helps them ($+2.2$\pp, $p=2\times10^{-9}$). On the Hit Predictor
the whole gain of artist history comes from the 18\,\% of songs by established artists, 97\,\% of which are hits,
which is the dataset effect mentioned above. Table and details: Appendix~\ref{app:robust}.


\subsection{Audit: which gains are not real}\label{sec:auditmain}
Several inputs gave large, significant gains that do not survive a closer look (Table~\ref{tab:survival}). The
\emph{song title} adds $+6.7$\pp, more than any musical input, because the titles of the non-hits are written
differently: on the genre-matched sample, 13.5\,\% of them carry a suffix such as ``- Remastered 2011'' or ``- Live'', against 0.1\,\% of the hits,
and genre words (\emph{blues, metal, jazz}) and non-English words mark the niche catalogue. Two controls measure
this. On the genre-matched sample (songs with both a preview and a Spotify genre, 20{,}610, from which as many hits
as flops are drawn inside every genre family: 7{,}554 songs; App.~\ref{app:audit}) the title still adds $+2.9$\pp{} to the final model; once suffixes, brackets and
``feat.'' are stripped, $+1.9$\pp{} ($p=0.002$, not significant after Bonferroni; App.~\ref{app:audit}). The title
reveals how the data were collected, not what makes a hit. A 185-dimensional \emph{librosa}
representation added $+3.8$\pp{} on one split ($p=0.002$) but $+0.9$\pp{} (n.s.) under cross-validation.
\emph{FFT} descriptors of 30-second previews add $+1.4$\pp{} on the whole benchmark ($p=4\times10^{-9}$), but nothing
($+0.1$\pp) when hits and non-hits come from the same genres: they recognise the excluded genres. The artist's
\emph{genre} itself would add $+10.1$\pp, but it is assigned by Spotify afterwards. \emph{Release year} looks strong on
ISMIR ($+3.1$\pp) only because hits there are older than the non-hit pool. \emph{Market-trend} features
(Sec.~\ref{sec:timeseries}), \texttt{chorus\_hit}/\texttt{sections} and title length add nothing. Every test, table
and figure of the audit is in Appendix~\ref{app:audit}.


\subsection{Lyrics on ISMIR 2019}\label{sec:lyrics}
ISMIR songs have lyrics in the official musiXmatch release (word counts over 5{,}000 words). We add
two input blocks (9 numbers) to the final ISMIR model: eight lexical features (log number of words, log number of distinct words,
richness, repetition, and the shares of \emph{I/me/my}, \emph{you/your}, \emph{love} and common English words) and the probability given by a bag-of-words model trained inside each fold.
Two shortcuts are removed first: only songs \emph{with} lyrics are used on both sides (56\,\% of hits have lyrics,
36\,\% of non-hits), and the test is repeated on English songs only (97.5\,\% of hits, 80.5\,\% of non-hits). With
year-matched negatives (protocol B, the one on which ISMIR inputs are judged, Sec.~\ref{sec:selection}) lyrics add
$+5.1$\pp{} ($p=2\times10^{-5}$), and still $+4.4$\pp{} with English songs only and no artist shared between
training and test ($p=3\times10^{-5}$, 10/10 folds; Fig.~\ref{fig:lyrics}a). With random negatives (protocol A) the
gains are $+4.7$\pp{} ($p=3\times10^{-4}$) and $+2.7$ to $+3.0$\pp{} in the English and unseen-artist variants
($p=6\times10^{-4}$): consistent, but only indicative under Bonferroni. The words with the largest weights (Fig.~\ref{fig:lyrics}b) are \emph{love}, vocables
(\emph{la, oh, yeah}) and direct address on the hit side, and darker words (\emph{kill, hate, crime}) on the other:
lyrics partly identify mainstream themes and genres. Details: Appendix~\ref{app:lyrics}.

\subsection{Time series: chart dynamics}\label{sec:timeseries}

Treating the chart as a time series answers two questions. \emph{Before release}, we built
market-trend features (how close a song's audio is to the previous decade's hits and to the direction in which
hits are moving): they add nothing significant to the final model ($+0.07$\pp, $p=0.66$; the 13 per-descriptor deviations
$+0.46$\pp, $p=0.006$, indicative) because they mostly encode the era.
The chart itself is a time series too: the number of weekly debuts is mostly trend, the number of holiday songs
almost purely seasonal; their decomposition, periodogram and ARIMA forecasts
are in Appendix~\ref{app:tsdetails}. \emph{After release}, the question changes.


\textbf{After release: early trajectories.} A different question can be asked once a song
has entered the chart: will it become a big hit? We take the 31{,}925 songs with a complete
history (debut after the first chart and at least 52 weeks of follow-up; 16.4\,\% reach
the top~10) and describe each by its first $k$ weeks on the chart. Concretely, ``the first
$k$ weeks'' are the $k$ chart positions the song holds in the $k$ weekly charts that start with
its debut: week~1 is the debut chart, week~2 the next week's chart, and so on, with position 101
if the song has dropped off. Madonna's \textit{Vogue}, for example, debuted at 39, then held 28
and 12 (so for $k=3$ the input is $(39, 28, 12)$) and later peaked at number~1. From these
positions we also compute the best rank so far, the average climb per week (slope), the change
of the weekly move (acceleration) and the number of weeks on the chart, and we add as context the
year, month, and the artist's earlier songs and earlier top-10 hits reached before the debut
(Random Forest, 200 trees, $5{\times}3$ CV). Table~\ref{tab:traj} shows that the trajectory dominates: three weeks plus
context give an AUC of 0.892 (the three weeks alone 0.845) against 0.593 for the context alone, and context adds $+0.03$ to $+0.05$ on
top (15/15 folds, $p<10^{-6}$ for every $k$). The prediction is not limited to songs already
at the top: among the songs not yet in the top~10 after three weeks, the AUC is 0.870, and a
model trained on songs that debuted before 2000 reaches 0.852 on songs from 2000 onwards ($k=3$, with context, the same 200-tree forest fitted once). As for online
content~\cite{szabo2010predicting}, early popularity predicts later popularity. This task
differs from that of Sec.~\ref{sec:main}: every song has already charted, so it separates
big hits from small ones and cannot be used before release. A second target, at least
20~weeks on the chart, depends on chart rules that remove older songs and is not reported.

\textbf{What happens in the first three weeks.} Fig.~\ref{fig:traj} shows why so few weeks are
enough. Songs that will reach the top~10 already differ at their debut (median position 73 against
87), but the gap opens in the following weeks: their median position climbs to 53 in week~2 and 39
in week~3, while the other songs stay around 80 and one in four has already left the chart by
week~3 (Fig.~\ref{fig:traj}a). The climb matters as much as the starting point
(Fig.~\ref{fig:traj}b): a song that debuts between 61 and 80 and loses ground reaches the top~10
3\,\% of the time, but 55\,\% of the time if it climbs 30 places or more by week~3. Even among debuts in
the top~40, the share is 28\,\% for songs that fall and 82--100\,\% for songs that climb at least ten
places. Permutation importance of the $k=3$ model (Fig.~\ref{fig:traj}c, held-out AUC 0.893) confirms
this. Shuffling the week-3 position costs 0.088 AUC, the climb per week 0.029 and the best rank 0.025,
whereas the debut position alone costs 0.004 once the later weeks are known. Among the context inputs,
only the year matters (0.023), because chart dynamics changed over time; the artist's earlier songs cost
less than 0.01. After release, then, it is the song's own momentum, not the artist's past, that tells a big
hit from a small one.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{The final model.} After the audit, the model we retain is the main model of
Sec.~\ref{sec:main}: the frozen Random Forest (80 trees, 8 candidate features per split) on 20
inputs per song, namely the 13 Spotify descriptors, six decade indicators and artist history
$h(s)$. It reaches 0.832 accuracy and 0.918 ROC-AUC on the Hit Predictor
(Table~\ref{tab:main}). On the external datasets the same recipe is used with each dataset's
own audio descriptors, the release year or decade and artist history, with a 200-tree Random
Forest (0.763 on ISMIR protocol~A, 0.708 on protocol~B, ROC-AUC 0.777 on SpotGenTrack).
XGBoost is 1.3 points more accurate under cross-validation (0.845 vs 0.832, untested) but ties with the Random Forest on
the canonical split ($p=0.87$), so we keep the configuration that matches prior work. Every
other candidate is left out for a stated reason (Table~\ref{tab:final}). A separate model
answers a different question, for songs already on the chart: the same kind of Random Forest
on the first weeks of the chart trajectory (Sec.~\ref{sec:timeseries}). Where lyrics are available
(ISMIR, songs with musiXmatch lyrics), adding them gives the best model we found on those songs (0.775 in
protocol~A, 0.727 in protocol~B, against 0.728 and 0.676 for the final model on the same songs;
Table~\ref{tab:lyrics}).

\textbf{How much of the gain is real?} The benchmark's negative class excludes the artists
of that decade's hits. Our feature only counts \emph{earlier} decades, so it is leakage-free
in time, but an artist who charted before is likely to chart again and is therefore
unlikely to appear as a negative by construction: flops have on average 0.037 earlier hits,
against 3.21 for hits. The $+5.0$\pp{} on this benchmark, and the head-to-head gains of
Table~\ref{tab:tilburg}, should be read as benchmark-specific. The ISMIR year-matched
protocol, whose negatives are chosen neither by artist nor by date, gives a more
conservative $+2.6$\pp; this is the number we consider transferable.

\textbf{Era as a shortcut.} On ISMIR, release year looks like a strong predictor (70.2\,\%
alone, and $+12$ to $+13$ points in the original study) because hits are older than the
non-hit pool. Once years are matched, it carries no information. On the Hit Predictor, the
decade is uninformative alone and helps only through interactions, which is consistent
with era-specific tastes. It could also reflect decade-dependent genre rules of the negative class, but on the
genre-matched sample the decade still adds $+2.5$\pp{} to the 13 descriptors (25/25, $p=8\times10^{-7}$;
Table~\ref{tab:genre}), so genre is not the whole story. Reported gains from date features should be accompanied by a year-matched control.

\textbf{Genre as a shortcut.} On the Hit Predictor, adding the artist's genre takes the
main model to $0.928$, and the apparent FFT gain disappears once genre is controlled
(Sec.~\ref{sec:further}). Any audio representation evaluated on this benchmark can score
by recognising the excluded genres rather than by modelling what makes a song a hit, so
audio gains on it should be reported together with a genre-matched control. Artist history
passes that control: on the genre-matched sample, decade and artist history still add $+6.7$\pp{} to the 13
descriptors (25/25, $p=9\times10^{-13}$; Table~\ref{tab:genre}). FFT does not. Within the audio content itself, therefore, no
representation we tested adds to artist history and era once the construction shortcuts
are controlled. The one content input that does is the lyrics (Sec.~\ref{sec:lyrics}), whose
gain survives year matching, an English-only control and unseen artists; their strongest words,
however, suggest that they too partly identify mainstream genres and themes, and a genre-matched control
for lyrics remains to be done. On these data, the 13 audio descriptors carry most of the accuracy, but what
adds to them and survives the controls is who sings the song, when, and what it is about---not a richer
description of how it sounds.

\textbf{Single splits and multiple comparisons.} Several of our own earlier single-split conclusions
did not survive cross-validation, and with a few hundred $p$-values some small ones are expected by chance; this is why
the survival ranking requires the Bonferroni threshold. Details: Appendix~\ref{app:stats}.

\textbf{Survival ranking.} Table~\ref{tab:survival} lists every gain we tested, split into three groups.
The groups are assigned in this order. \emph{Did not survive}: $p$ above the Bonferroni threshold, whatever the
control (a failed control is then noted in the verdict). \emph{Shortcut} (leaks included): significant after Bonferroni, but
failing a control (under at least one control its gain is no longer Bonferroni-significant) or not known on
release day, so the gain is real on that dataset but would not transfer. \emph{Survived}: significant after
Bonferroni and failing none of the controls run on it (a \checkmark{} where a control was run). Only artist history, era, the lyrics and, after release, the early
trajectory are in the survived group. The table groups by outcome; Fig.~\ref{fig:selection} instead lists each
rejected input at the first question it fails, so an input that fails both a control and Bonferroni (release year
on ISMIR, the SpotGenTrack leak) appears in the figure under ``shortcut'' or ``leak'' and in the table under ``did
not survive'' with the failed control noted.


\textbf{Limitations.} Artist history is matched by normalised name, not by a canonical
identifier, so homonymous artists can be merged. On SpotGenTrack the ``hits'' are songs with Spotify popularity
$\geq55$, a score measured when the dataset was collected, not at release; artist history there counts
the artist's earlier songs that are popular \emph{today}, so it is not strictly known on release day, and the
SpotGenTrack gain should be read as an upper bound. The Hit Predictor and ISMIR results do not have this problem. Artist history does not help new artists on
the Hit Predictor and at most modestly on ISMIR ($+1.4$\pp, indicative), and it partly measures
cumulative advantage~\cite{salganik2006experimental} rather than the intrinsic appeal
of a song. We use classical classifiers on fixed descriptors rather than deep networks on
raw audio~\cite{yang2017revisiting,nofer2026deephits}: our question is which inputs carry
signal under a controlled protocol, and the comparison with prior work is made at equal
model; a learned representation could still capture what the descriptors miss. The
comparison with Zangerle et~al.\ uses a classifier, whereas they trained a regression on peak chart position
(non-hits set to 150) and report as accuracy the share of songs placed on the right side of position 100; we
also use a different random draw of negatives; the comparison with Du uses re-drawn
splits. On ISMIR the period of a song is its release year in the Million Song Dataset, which for a reissue or a
compilation can be later than its chart run, so ``earlier'' hits there are only as reliable as that year. Our audio
previews are 30-second clips from a different service than the one that
computed the Spotify descriptors.

\section{Future Work}

Sec.~\ref{sec:further} already runs the cheap versions of several follow-ups: artist
matching (no gain available), a genre control for audio features, model ageing across decades and
lexical lyrics descriptors. What remains needs new data.
(i) \emph{Cold start}: artist history barely helps first-time artists on the benchmark, and
the FFT gain for them was a genre shortcut; features such as label, collaborators, prior
streams or social media remain to be tested, with a genre-matched control.
(ii) \emph{Lyrics and emotion}: lyrics for the Hit Predictor songs (matched by title and
artist), a genre-matched control for the lyrics gain, semantic and affective lyrics representations, and learned
audio embeddings, evaluated with the same audit protocol.
(iii) \emph{Benchmark design}: a hit-prediction dataset whose negatives are sampled
independently of artist, genre and date. Our genre-matched sample is a small, post-hoc
approximation of it; a purpose-built one would let content features be compared without
construction shortcuts.
(iv) \emph{Early trajectories}: Sec.~\ref{sec:timeseries} uses weekly chart positions only;
daily streams and radio plays would allow the same prediction days after release.

\section{Conclusion}
A song's artist history is a simple, leakage-free signal that improves hit prediction
across classifiers, beats the only prior model on the Spotify Hit Predictor benchmark in a
head-to-head comparison, and transfers to an independent peer-reviewed dataset where it
exceeds the best published accuracy on the original protocol and survives a year-matched control. These gains hold
for artists the model never saw in training, which rules out artist recognition; but artist
history does not help an artist with no earlier hit on the benchmark, where for those newcomers only era context
helps (on ISMIR there is an indicative hint, $p=0.002$, that an empty history is informative); spectral descriptors of a 30-second
preview appear to help them too, but that gain disappears under a genre control. The same audit
shows that larger gains---song titles, release dates, genre-driven audio features, rich
audio on a single split---can
come from how the data were built or from chance. Chart time series add nothing before
release, but after release a song's first weeks are the strongest predictor we found. The lyrics are
the only content input whose gain survives every control we could run on them (year matching, English only,
unseen artists). Knowing which gains are real matters more
than the headline number.
