What really predicts a hit?

The story of our project: what we tried, what worked, and what turned out to be a trap.

Technion – Israel Institute of Technology · Project in Artificial Intelligence

The question

Can a computer guess, before a song comes out, whether it will be a hit?

What is a hit?

A song that entered the Billboard Hot 100, the weekly chart of the most popular songs in the USA.

How the computer learns

We show it thousands of songs whose answer we already know. It finds patterns. Then we test it on songs it has never seen.

What is a good score?

Guessing at random gives 50 % right, like tossing a coin. Everything we do is compared to that.

Method · how every gain is tested

Two estimates for every comparison — and a test that is not fooled by overlap

1 · Canonical split

The split of our earlier experiments (seed 42; 20,000 train / 2,000 test). Two models compared song by song with McNemar’s test.

train 20,000

Reproduces the old numbers exactly: .773 → .822.

2 · Repeated cross-validation

5 folds × 5 repeats = 25 test sets. Mean, 95 % confidence interval, and the Nadeau–Bengio corrected t-test — the naive t-test is over-confident because the folds overlap.

= a fold where the gain is positive

Scaling, PCA and title models are fitted inside each training fold only.

Input · the decade (WHEN)

What is the decade input, and how do we build it?

What it is

The decade in which the song came out: 1960s, 70s, 80s, 90s, 2000s or 2010s.

How we build it

The dataset comes as 6 files, one per decade (dataset-of-60s.csv … dataset-of-10s.csv). The decade = the file. We turn it into 6 yes/no boxes, exactly one is 1 (one-hot), because a decade is a category, not a number.

Why it helps

Each decade is half hits, so alone it is a coin toss (50.3 %). But what a hit sounds like changes with the era (right): with the decade, the trees learn one rule per era. +2.0 points.

Median of three sound numbers for hits (blue) and non-hits (gray). A 2000s non-hit is louder than any 1960s–80s hit; hits were less acoustic than non-hits until the 1990s, slightly more after; hits were longer in the 1970s–90s, shorter after 2000.

Input · artist history (WHO)

What is artist history, and how do we compute it?

Rule

For a song, take its artist and count their different songs on the Billboard Hot 100 in earlier decades. Never the same decade, never the future, so it is known on release day.

Formula

h = log(1 + count). The log squeezes big counts: 40 earlier hits is not twice 20.

For every artist

Names are cleaned (lower case, accents, “feat.” removed) and matched to the full Hot 100 history (1958–2026). Computed separately for every artist and every decade.

Step 4 · A surprise

A trap: how the non-hits were chosen

The dataset's own rules: a non-hit must come from an artist who had no hit that decade, in a niche genre (jazz, Latin, classical...).

So “the artist had hits before” is partly the dataset's rule, not the music.

97%

of songs by an artist with an earlier hit are hits in this dataset. Our +5 is too optimistic here.

Two different questions

“Unseen” artist ≠ “new” artist

Unseen artist — not in training

The model never trained on this artist's songs, but the artist may have earlier hits (h > 0). We test it by putting each artist only in training or only in test.

Result: the gain stays — +5.0 on the big dataset, +2.9 on the fair ISMIR test. So the model does not just recognise artists.

New artist — no earlier hit

h = 0 by definition, so artist history cannot help (+0.4, not significant). Only the decade helps them (+2.2).

On the big dataset, the whole gain of artist history comes from the 18 % of songs by known artists — 97 % of them are hits.

Step 6 · Time series and hits

Can time series predict a hit? It depends on when you ask

BEFORE the song comes out (our question)

Does the song sound like the recent hits? +0.1, nothing: the trend only knows the decade, which we already had. ✗ removed

No

AFTER it comes out (another question)

The song just entered the chart. Its first 3 weeks tell if it will reach the top 10: right 89 times out of 100 when comparing two songs (AUC .89). ✓ kept

Yes

Both are true. Time series do not help guess a hit before it is out — but once it is out, they are the best clue we found.

After release · the first weeks on the chart

What happens in the first 3 weeks decides a top-10 hit

First k weeks = the song's chart position in each of its first k weeks (101 = dropped off). Vogue: 39 → 28 → 12, then no. 1.

Future top-10s climb fast: median 73 → 53 → 39. The others stay near 80. Debut 61–80 and falling: 3 %; climbing 30+ places: 55 %.

The model uses the week-3 position and the climb, not the debut. AUC .89 with 3 weeks, .59 without.

Summary

What we proved

TRUE — IT SURVIVED EVERY TEST

1 · Who sings it matters: +5 points on the big dataset, +2.6 on a fair test.

2 · We beat the published results: 76.3 % vs 75.0 %, and the other study 50 times out of 50.

3 · It works with any algorithm and for artists never seen.

4 · For a brand-new artist, only the era helps a little.

5 · Lyrics add +4.4 on a fair test, with unseen artists.

6 · After a song comes out, its first 3 weeks predict a top-10 hit (AUC .89). Before, time series add nothing.

FALSE — IT WAS A TRAP

Song titles: reveal how the data was collected.

Detailed sound: the gain was luck.

FFT: it was recognising the genre.

The year: hits were just older songs.

Music trends: they only know the decade.

In the sound itself, nothing survived every test — the lyrics did.

A hit is mostly who sings it, when and what it is about — and, once it is out, how its first weeks go.

How it works

How the computer learns, and how we test it

A Random Forest = 80 small decision trees

Each tree asks simple questions — is it danceable? is it long? is it acoustic? — and votes “hit” or “not hit”. The majority wins. We never tune it, so any improvement comes from the clues we give it, not from tweaking.

We test it 25 times

We hide 1/5 of the songs, train on the rest, and guess the hidden ones. Then again with other hidden songs: 25 tests in total. An improvement counts only if it appears again and again, and passes a statistical test.

Step 5 · The FFT

The FFT looked like a real gain — it was recognising the genre

It looked good

The FFT splits the sound into frequencies (bass, middle, treble). +1.4 points on 35,000 songs, very significant.

Why it was a trap

Non-hits come from niche genres (rule of the dataset), and the sound tells the genre. The computer was recognising jazz or Latin, not hits.

Fair test

Same number of hits and non-hits in every genre: +0.1, nothing. ✗ removed

Audit · spectral features (FFT) on the whole benchmark

FFT looked like a real gain for new artists — it was the genre

20,610 songs with a clip and a Spotify genre · 5×5 CV · paper Table X

On 35,370 songs, FFT adds +1.4 (25/25 folds, p = 4·10⁻⁹), all of it on new artists.

But Latin, classical, jazz, world and metal artists make 50 % of the non-hits and 1.9 % of the hits — by construction. Audio can recognise genre (FFT: 49.9 % vs 26.1 % by chance).

With genre known, FFT lowers accuracy (−1.1). On a genre-matched sample (as many hits as non-hits in every genre): +0.1, p = .84. remove

Genre alone lifts accuracy to 92.8 %, but Spotify assigns genres afterwards, so we use it only as a control.

What is new

Artist history is not a new idea. Proving what it is worth is.

ALREADY EXISTED

Interiano et al. (2018): a “superstar” indicator (did the artist chart recently?) improves a Random Forest on 500,000 UK songs.

Middlebrook & Sheik (2019) list an artist past-performance field, but never isolate or test it.

Neither tests significance, compares on the same data, or checks for sampling artefacts.

What this study cannot say

LIMITATIONS

  • Artists matched by name, not by a canonical ID (homonyms)
  • No deep-learning model: we compare inputs at equal model
  • Artist history partly measures cumulative advantage, not a song’s appeal
  • ISMIR: a Random Forest and a different draw of negatives, not their network
  • Du: re-drawn splits, since the original R seed cannot be replayed
  • Audio clips come from Deezer, not from Spotify
