The problem

Hit-prediction papers report 86 %, 88 %, 89 % — and none of these numbers can be compared

≠

Different datasets

Each study builds its own hits and non-hits. An 88 % on one set says nothing about another.

1×

One split, no test

Gains are measured on a single train/test split, usually without a significance test.

⚠

Shortcuts

A feature can separate hits from non-hits because of how the negatives were chosen, not because of anything that makes a song succeed.

Our question: which gains are real — and how big are they once all three problems are controlled?

Method · the starting point

We start from the 13 audio descriptors that Spotify computes for every song

danceabilityenergykeyloudnessmodespeechinessacousticnessinstrumentalnesslivenessvalencetempodurationtime signature

Each song is a row of 13 numbers; the label is 1 = hit (on the weekly Hot 100 in its decade), 0 = non-hit. The task is binary classification.

The dataset also has chorus_hit and sections; we test them in Part 5 and they add nothing.

Method · the two signals

Two cheap, non-audio signals added to the 13 Spotify audio features

ERA

Decade / release year

A hit in the 1960s does not sound like a hit in the 2010s. The era lets the model apply era-specific rules.

One-hot decade on the Hit Predictor; year or decade as a number elsewhere.

ARTIST HISTORY

Earlier Hot 100 songs

h(s) = log(1 + #hits by the same artist strictly before the song’s period)

Strictly earlier periods only → no information from the song’s own period or the future.

Artist names normalised (accents, “feat.”, “&” removed). 99.99 % of hits are found in the chart history.

Method · the other algorithms

Feature extraction, explanation and statistics

TURNING AUDIO INTO FEATURES

librosa: 185 descriptors from 30-second clips (timbre, rhythm, harmony).

FFT: 31 spectral descriptors (energy per frequency band).

PCA: compresses the 185 librosa descriptors to 40 components.

Essentia: the 592 descriptors of the ISMIR dataset.

TEXT AND EXPLANATION

TF-IDF + logistic regression: turns the song title into one probability, trained out-of-fold so the test songs are never seen.

TreeSHAP: shows which features the Random Forest relies on: artist history #1, decade #2, instrumentalness #3.

TESTING EVERY GAIN

McNemar: compares two models song by song on one split.

Corrected t-test (Nadeau–Bengio): compares two models over 25 folds without over-confidence.

Bonferroni: ~60 tests, so a result needs p < 8·10⁻⁴ to count as solid.

Method · how every gain is tested

Two estimates for every comparison — and a test that is not fooled by overlap

1 · Canonical split

The split of our earlier experiments (seed 42; 20,000 train / 2,000 test). Two models compared song by song with McNemar’s test.

train 20,000

Reproduces the old numbers exactly: .773 → .822.

2 · Repeated cross-validation

5 folds × 5 repeats = 25 test sets. Mean, 95 % confidence interval, and the Nadeau–Bengio corrected t-test — the naive t-test is over-confident because the folds overlap.

= a fold where the gain is positive

Scaling, PCA and title models are fitted inside each training fold only.

The catch · how the benchmark was built

The Kaggle dataset chose its non-hits partly by artist

DATASET README — A “FLOP” MUST SATISFY ALL OF

  1. The track is not in that decade’s hit list.
  2. The track’s artist is not in that decade’s hit list.
  3. It belongs to a “non-mainstream and/or avant-garde” genre.
  4. That genre has no song in the hit list.

96.6%

of songs by an artist with an earlier hit are hits. On this benchmark, “known artist” almost means “hit”.

So the +5.0 is a benchmark-specific upper bound. We need data whose negatives are not chosen by artist → ISMIR 2019.

Audit · which gains are not real

A big, significant gain can still be wrong: artefact or luck

single split5×5 cross-validation

Gain over audio in points · top: full benchmark · bottom: 3,562-song audio subset · paper Table IX

artefact Song titles +6.7, more than any musical feature. Titles don’t make hits — they reveal that non-hits come from niche genres and catalogue releases.

luck Rich librosa audio: +3.8 (p = .002) on one split, +0.9, not significant in CV. Same for “PCA 40 is the peak” and the family knock-outs.

chorus_hit + sections, often suspected of leakage, add nothing. FFT survives here — the next slide shows why it should not.

Audit · spectral features (FFT) on the whole benchmark

FFT looked like a real gain for new artists — it was the genre

20,610 songs with a clip and a Spotify genre · 5×5 CV · paper Table X

On 35,370 songs, FFT adds +1.4 (25/25 folds, p = 4·10⁻⁹), all of it on new artists.

But Latin, classical, jazz, world and metal artists make 50 % of the non-hits and 1.9 % of the hits — by construction. Audio can recognise genre (FFT: 49.9 % vs 26.1 % by chance).

With genre known, FFT lowers accuracy (−1.1). On a genre-matched sample (as many hits as non-hits in every genre): +0.1, p = .84. remove

Genre alone lifts accuracy to 92.8 %, but Spotify assigns genres afterwards, so we use it only as a control.

Statistics · about 60 p-values in one study

With many tests, some pass by chance — so we apply Bonferroni: p < 0.05 / 60 ≈ 8·10⁻⁴

Time series · the Hot 100 as a signal (our supervisor's reading plan)

Trend, seasonality, residual — and which forecasting algorithms work

COMPONENTS · 68 YEARS OF WEEKLY CHARTS

Debuts per week: mostly trend (strength .72): 11 in 1959, 5.5 in 2001, 11 again with streaming. Seasonality weak (.11): January is quiet. Largest residual spike: a rule change in December 1998.

Holiday songs on the chart: almost pure seasonality. The FFT finds the 52-week cycle and its harmonics (26, 17.4, 13 weeks) without being told; a Fourier fit follows the streaming-era peaks (R² = .94).

Notebook timeseries/time_series_hot100.ipynb: STL decomposition, ACF/PACF, ADF/KPSS, Fourier terms.

What is new

Artist history is not a new idea. Proving what it is worth is.

ALREADY EXISTED

Interiano et al. (2018): a “superstar” indicator (did the artist chart recently?) improves a Random Forest on 500,000 UK songs.

Middlebrook & Sheik (2019) list an artist past-performance field, but never isolate or test it.

Neither tests significance, compares on the same data, or checks for sampling artefacts.

OUR CONTRIBUTION

  • A leakage-free version (strictly earlier periods)
  • A significance-tested head-to-head on the same data as prior work
  • External validation with a year-matched control
  • Unseen-artist and cold-start analysis
  • An audit of artefacts and single-split luck

Limitations and next steps

What this study cannot say

LIMITATIONS

  • Artists matched by name, not by a canonical ID (homonyms)
  • No deep-learning model: we compare inputs at equal model
  • Artist history partly measures cumulative advantage, not a song’s appeal
  • ISMIR: a Random Forest and a different draw of negatives, not their network
  • Du: re-drawn splits, since the original R seed cannot be replayed
  • Audio clips come from Deezer, not from Spotify

NEXT

  • Features that help first-time artists: label, collaborators, early streams — always with a genre-matched control
  • Semantic lyrics and learned audio embeddings, under the same audit
  • Early-trajectory forecasting (Part 6) with streaming data
  • A benchmark whose negatives ignore artist, genre and date
