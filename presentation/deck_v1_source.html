What really predicts a hit?

Artist history, era context and shortcut features — tested on four datasets, with every gain checked for chance and for artefacts.

Lior Shalon & Nathanael Uzzan

Technion – Israel Institute of Technology · Project in Artificial Intelligence

The problem

Hit-prediction papers report 86 %, 88 %, 89 % — and none of these numbers can be compared

≠

Different datasets

Each study builds its own hits and non-hits. An 88 % on one set says nothing about another.

1×

One split, no test

Gains are measured on a single train/test split, usually without a significance test.

⚠

Shortcuts

A feature can separate hits from non-hits because of how the negatives were chosen, not because of anything that makes a song succeed.

Our question: which gains are real — and how big are they once all three problems are controlled?

Method · how every gain is tested

Two estimates for every comparison — and a test that is not fooled by overlap

1 · Canonical split

The split of our earlier experiments (seed 42; 20,000 train / 2,000 test). Two models compared song by song with McNemar’s test.

train 20,000

Reproduces the old numbers exactly: .773 → .822.

2 · Repeated cross-validation

5 folds × 5 repeats = 25 test sets. Mean, 95 % confidence interval, and the Nadeau–Bengio corrected t-test — the naive t-test is over-confident because the folds overlap.

= a fold where the gain is positive

The catch · how the benchmark was built

The Kaggle dataset chose its non-hits partly by artist

DATASET README — A “FLOP” MUST SATISFY ALL OF

  1. The track is not in that decade’s hit list.
  2. The track’s artist is not in that decade’s hit list.
  3. It belongs to a “non-mainstream and/or avant-garde” genre.
  4. That genre has no song in the hit list.

Audit · which gains are not real

A big, significant gain can still be wrong: artefact or luck

single split5×5 cross-validation

Gain over audio in points · top: full benchmark · bottom: 3,562-song audio subset · paper Table IX

artefact Song titles +6.7, more than any musical feature. Titles don’t make hits — they reveal that non-hits come from niche genres and catalogue releases.

luck Rich librosa audio: +3.8 (p = .002) on one split, +0.9, not significant in CV. Same for “PCA 40 is the peak” and the family knock-outs.

chorus_hit + sections, often suspected of leakage, add nothing.

Statistics · about 60 p-values in one study

With many tests, some pass by chance — so we apply Bonferroni: p < 0.05 / 60 ≈ 8·10⁻⁴

SURVIVES — OUR CONCLUSIONS REST ON THESE

  • Main gains on the benchmark (p = 7·10⁻¹⁷)
  • All five classifiers
  • Head-to-head with Du, all four models
  • Temporal hold-out on unseen decades
  • Artist history on ISMIR, protocols A and B
  • Artist-grouped gains

INDICATIVE ONLY — 8·10⁻⁴ < p < 0.05

  • Decade and FFT gains on the small audio subset
  • Most single-split gains on that subset
  • SpotGenTrack accuracy, artist alone (p = 10⁻³)
  • ISMIR cold-start gains (p = .002, .003)

What is new

Artist history is not a new idea. Proving what it is worth is.

ALREADY EXISTED

Middlebrook & Sheik (2019) list an artist past-performance field among chart-derived fields — but their final inputs are not stated and its contribution is never isolated or tested.

OUR CONTRIBUTION

  • A leakage-free version (strictly earlier periods)
  • A significance-tested head-to-head on the same data as prior work
  • External validation with a year-matched control
  • Unseen-artist and cold-start analysis
  • An audit of artefacts and single-split luck

Limitations and next steps

What this study cannot say

LIMITATIONS

  • Artist names match for only 52 % of benchmark songs
  • Artist history partly measures cumulative advantage, not a song’s appeal
  • ISMIR: a Random Forest and a different draw of negatives, not their network
  • Du: re-drawn splits, since the original R seed cannot be replayed
  • Audio clips come from Deezer, not from Spotify

NEXT

  • Canonical artist IDs (MusicBrainz / Spotify) to raise the match rate
  • Features that help first-time artists: label, collaborators, social media
  • Rolling-origin evaluation across all decades
  • Lyrics and learned audio embeddings, under the same audit
  • A benchmark whose negatives ignore artist, genre and date
