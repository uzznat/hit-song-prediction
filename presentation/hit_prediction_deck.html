Hit song prediction · Technion · Project in AI

What really predicts a hit?

The story of our project: what we tried, what worked, and what turned out to be a trap.

Lior Shalon & Nathanael Uzzan

Technion – Israel Institute of Technology · Project in Artificial Intelligence

The question

Can a computer guess, before a song comes out, whether it will be a hit?

What is a hit?

A song that entered the Billboard Hot 100, the weekly chart of the most popular songs in the USA.

How the computer learns

We show it thousands of songs whose answer we already know. It finds patterns. Then we test it on songs it has never seen.

What is a good score?

Guessing at random gives 50 % right, like tossing a coin. Everything we do is compared to that.

→ We started with the most obvious clue: the sound of the song.

Step 1

We started with the sound

Spotify describes every song with 13 numbers: how danceable, how energetic, how fast, how loud, how acoustic, how happy it sounds...

danceabilityenergytempoloudnessacousticnessvalence (happy)+ 7 more

78%

right, on 40,560 songs (half hits, half not). Much better than a coin, but 1 song in 5 is still wrong.

→ But how does the computer learn, and how do we know the score is not luck?

How it works

How the computer learns, and how we test it

A Random Forest = 80 small decision trees

Each tree asks simple questions — is it danceable? is it long? is it acoustic? — and votes “hit” or “not hit”. The majority wins. We never tune it, so any improvement comes from the clues we give it, not from tweaking.

We test it 25 times

We hide 1/5 of the songs, train on the rest, and guess the hidden ones. Then again with other hidden songs: 25 tests in total. An improvement counts only if it appears again and again, and passes a statistical test.

→ The sound alone was not enough. So we asked: what else makes a hit?

Step 2 · Our idea

A hit is not only a sound — it is also who sings it and when

WHO · the artist's history

Has this artist had hits before? We count their Hot 100 songs in earlier decades only, never the future — the computer could know this on the day the song comes out.

WHEN · the decade

A hit in 1965 does not sound like a hit in 2015. Knowing the decade lets the computer use the right “taste” for each era.

→ We added these two clues to the 13 sound numbers.

Step 2 · Result

From 78 % to 83 % right

+5points

Better in 25 out of 25 tests. Each clue helps on its own; together they help the most.

→ Why does “who” work so well?

Step 2 · Why it works

One earlier hit changes everything

Share of songs that are hits, by the artist's number of earlier hits

Artist with no earlier hit: 39 % of their songs are hits.

With 1 or 2 earlier hits: 91 %.

→ +5 points is a lot. Too much? We tried hard to prove it was luck.

Step 3

Is it luck? We tried to break it — it held

5 different algorithms

Random Forest, XGBoost, SVM, k-NN, logistic regression: all gain +4.6 to +5.7. It is the clues, not the algorithm.

Every decade

From the 1960s to the 2010s: +3.7 to +6.6 every time.

The future

Trained on old decades, tested on a decade it never saw: still +1.9 to +3.4.

The only other study

We redid its exact method (Du, 2022) and added our clue: better in 50 out of 50 tries.

→ It was not luck. But then we read how the dataset had been built...

Step 4 · A surprise

A trap: how the non-hits were chosen

The dataset's own rules: a non-hit must come from an artist who had no hit that decade, in a niche genre (jazz, Latin, classical...).

So “the artist had hits before” is partly the dataset's rule, not the music.

97%

of songs by an artist with an earlier hit are hits in this dataset. Our +5 is too optimistic here.

→ So we needed a dataset built differently.

Step 4 · Another dataset

We tested on another dataset, built without that rule

ISMIR 2019 dataset (published, 11,662 songs): non-hits are random songs.

76.3%

with our clue — better than the best published result (75.0 %).

But another trap

The year alone already gets 70 %: the hits in this dataset are older songs. The computer can win just by looking at the date.

→ So we made the test fair: every hit against a non-hit from the same year.

Step 4 · The fair test

Same year for hits and non-hits: the date is useless, the artist still helps

The year

useless

It was only a trick of the dataset.

The artist's history

+2.6points

Better in 10 out of 10 tests. This is our honest number: smaller than +5, but real.

→ And for artists the computer has never seen?

Step 4 · New artists

Artists never seen, and brand-new artists

Artists never seen in training

We made sure no test artist appeared in training. The gain stays the same (+5.0): the computer does not just memorise names.

A brand-new artist (first song ever)

No history, so “who” cannot help (+0.4, nothing). “When” helps a little (+2.2). This is the hardest case — and the main thing left to solve.

→ Next, we tried to give the computer more musical information.

Step 5 · Adding more

We tried more clues — and checked every gain

Song titles: +6.7

Too good to be true. The titles reveal how the dataset was collected, not what makes a hit. ✗ removed

Detailed sound (185 measures)

+3.8 on one test... but only +0.9 over 25 tests, which is nothing. The first result was luck. ✗ removed

Small details

Length of the title, where the chorus starts, number of sections: nothing. ✗ removed

→ Then one clue looked really good: splitting the sound into frequencies (FFT).

Step 5 · The FFT

The FFT looked like a real gain — it was recognising the genre

It looked good

The FFT splits the sound into frequencies (bass, middle, treble). +1.4 points on 35,000 songs, very significant.

Why it was a trap

Non-hits come from niche genres (rule of the dataset), and the sound tells the genre. The computer was recognising jazz or Latin, not hits.

Fair test

Same number of hits and non-hits in every genre: +0.1, nothing. ✗ removed

→ Lesson: a big gain can be a trap. Then our teacher asked us to study time series.

Step 6 · Time series

We learned to read the chart as a signal over time

3 parts of a time series

Trend: slow change over years. Seasonality: repeats every year. Residual: what is left.

What we found

New songs per week: mostly a trend (fewer until 2001, more again with streaming). Christmas songs: pure seasonality — the FFT finds the 52-week cycle by itself.

Forecasting next year

ARIMA and Holt-Winters are the best ✓ kept. Machine learning is not better here: 800 data points are too few.

→ Then the big question: can time series help predict a hit?

Step 6 · Time series and hits

Can time series predict a hit? It depends on when you ask

BEFORE the song comes out (our question)

Does the song sound like the recent hits? +0.1, nothing: the trend only knows the decade, which we already had. ✗ removed

No

AFTER it comes out (another question)

The song just entered the chart. Its first 3 weeks tell if it will reach the top 10: right 89 times out of 100 when comparing two songs (AUC .89). ✓ kept

Yes

Both are true. Time series do not help guess a hit before it is out — but once it is out, they are the best clue we found.

→ So, what did we prove in the end?

Summary

What we proved

TRUE — IT SURVIVED EVERY TEST

1 · Who sings it matters: +5 points on the big dataset, +2.6 on a fair test.

2 · We beat the published results: 76.3 % vs 75.0 %, and the other study 50 times out of 50.

3 · It works with any algorithm and for artists never seen.

4 · For a brand-new artist, only the era helps a little.

5 · After a song comes out, its first 3 weeks predict a top-10 hit (AUC .89). Before, time series add nothing.

FALSE — IT WAS A TRAP

Song titles: reveal how the data was collected.

Detailed sound: the gain was luck.

FFT: it was recognising the genre.

The year: hits were just older songs.

Music trends: they only know the decade.

In the sound itself, nothing survived every test.

A hit is mostly who sings it and when — and, once it is out, how its first weeks go.

What is left

What we cannot do yet

Brand-new artists

With no history, we have almost nothing. Next: the record label, the collaborators, the first streams.

Lyrics and deep learning

Lyrics helped a little on one dataset (+0.6). Neural networks on the raw sound are still to try — with the same careful tests.

A dataset without traps

Every dataset we used had a trap (artist, genre or year). A fair one would make the question much cleaner.

Method · the other algorithms

Feature extraction, explanation and statistics

TURNING AUDIO INTO FEATURES

librosa: 185 descriptors from 30-second clips (timbre, rhythm, harmony).

FFT: 31 spectral descriptors (energy per frequency band).

PCA: compresses the 185 librosa descriptors to 40 components.

Essentia: the 592 descriptors of the ISMIR dataset.

TEXT AND EXPLANATION

TF-IDF + logistic regression: turns the song title into one probability, trained out-of-fold so the test songs are never seen.

TreeSHAP: shows which features the Random Forest relies on: artist history #1, decade #2, instrumentalness #3.

TESTING EVERY GAIN

McNemar: compares two models song by song on one split.

Corrected t-test (Nadeau–Bengio): compares two models over 25 folds without over-confidence.

Bonferroni: ~60 tests, so a result needs p < 8·10⁻⁴ to count as solid.

Method · how every gain is tested

Audit · which gains are not real

A big, significant gain can still be wrong: artefact or luck

single split5×5 cross-validation

Gain over audio in points · top: full benchmark · bottom: 3,562-song audio subset · paper Table IX

artefact Song titles +6.7, more than any musical feature. Titles don’t make hits — they reveal that non-hits come from niche genres and catalogue releases.

luck Rich librosa audio: +3.8 (p = .002) on one split, +0.9, not significant in CV. Same for “PCA 40 is the peak” and the family knock-outs.

chorus_hit + sections, often suspected of leakage, add nothing. FFT survives here — the next slide shows why it should not.

Audit · spectral features (FFT) on the whole benchmark

FFT looked like a real gain for new artists — it was the genre

20,610 songs with a clip and a Spotify genre · 5×5 CV · paper Table X

On 35,370 songs, FFT adds +1.4 (25/25 folds, p = 4·10⁻⁹), all of it on new artists.

But Latin, classical, jazz, world and metal artists make 50 % of the non-hits and 1.9 % of the hits — by construction. Audio can recognise genre (FFT: 49.9 % vs 26.1 % by chance).

With genre known, FFT lowers accuracy (−1.1). On a genre-matched sample (as many hits as non-hits in every genre): +0.1, p = .84. remove

Genre alone lifts accuracy to 92.8 %, but Spotify assigns genres afterwards, so we use it only as a control.

With many tests, some pass by chance — so we apply Bonferroni: p < 0.05 / 60 ≈ 8·10⁻⁴

What is new

Artist history is not a new idea. Proving what it is worth is.

ALREADY EXISTED

Interiano et al. (2018): a “superstar” indicator (did the artist chart recently?) improves a Random Forest on 500,000 UK songs.

Middlebrook & Sheik (2019) list an artist past-performance field, but never isolate or test it.

Neither tests significance, compares on the same data, or checks for sampling artefacts.

OUR CONTRIBUTION

  • A leakage-free version (strictly earlier periods)
  • A significance-tested head-to-head on the same data as prior work
  • External validation with a year-matched control
  • Unseen-artist and cold-start analysis
  • An audit of artefacts and single-split luck

Limitations and next steps

What this study cannot say

LIMITATIONS

  • Artists matched by name, not by a canonical ID (homonyms)
  • No deep-learning model: we compare inputs at equal model
  • Artist history partly measures cumulative advantage, not a song’s appeal
  • ISMIR: a Random Forest and a different draw of negatives, not their network
  • Du: re-drawn splits, since the original R seed cannot be replayed
  • Audio clips come from Deezer, not from Spotify

NEXT

  • Features that help first-time artists: label, collaborators, early streams — always with a genre-matched control
  • Semantic lyrics and learned audio embeddings, under the same audit
  • Early-trajectory forecasting (Chapter 6) with streaming data
  • A benchmark whose negatives ignore artist, genre and date
